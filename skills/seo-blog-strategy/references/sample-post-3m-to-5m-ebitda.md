# How Do I Get From $3M EBITDA to $5M EBITDA So I Can Sell to a Network?

If you're running a $3M EBITDA agency and eyeing a network exit, you already know the brutal truth: most agency holding companies and networks won't seriously look at you until you clear $5M. Some want $7M. A few won't return your call under $10M.

That $2M gap between where you are and where you need to be isn't just a number on a spreadsheet. It's 18-36 months of focused, deliberate work — and it determines whether you exit at a 6-8x multiple to a strategic buyer or a 3-4x multiple to a financial buyer who's buying you because they couldn't get into a better deal.

This guide walks through exactly how agency owners bridge that gap. Not theory. The actual levers, the math behind each one, and the mistakes that kill deals.

## Why $5M EBITDA Is the Magic Number

Agency networks and holdcos (S4 Capital, Stagwell, Plus Company, Next 15, and the roll-up PE platforms) have an internal deal-size threshold driven by three things:

1. **Deal economics**: Legal, due diligence, and integration costs run $500K-$1M+ per acquisition. Buying a $3M EBITDA agency means overhead eats 15-25% of the deal value. At $5M+, that same overhead drops below 10%.
2. **Platform logic**: Networks buy agencies to plug into existing platforms. A sub-$5M agency often can't support the reporting infrastructure, compliance, and integration work required.
3. **Multiple arbitrage**: Holdcos get paid at 10-14x EBITDA in public markets. They buy at 6-8x. The spread only works if the target is big enough to move the needle on their consolidated financials.

Below $5M, you're playing a different game — PE roll-ups, family offices, or selling to another agency. These buyers are legitimate, but the multiples are typically 4-6x instead of 7-10x.

So the $3M to $5M journey isn't just about adding $2M in profit. It's about unlocking a different buyer pool with 2x better multiples.

## The Math: What $2M More in EBITDA Actually Requires

Most owners massively underestimate how much top-line growth is needed to add $2M in EBITDA. Here's the reality by margin profile:

| Current EBITDA margin | Revenue needed to add $2M EBITDA |
|----------------------|----------------------------------|
| 15% | $13.3M new revenue |
| 20% | $10M new revenue |
| 25% | $8M new revenue |
| 30% | $6.6M new revenue |

If your agency is doing $15M revenue at 20% EBITDA margin ($3M), you need to get to roughly $25M revenue at the same margin to hit $5M EBITDA. That's a 67% revenue increase while holding margins steady — which almost never happens, because growth typically compresses margins before it expands them.

The better path is almost always a combination: grow revenue AND improve margins simultaneously. If you can push margins from 20% to 25% while growing revenue from $15M to $20M, you land at $5M EBITDA without needing a massive top-line expansion.

## The 7 Levers to Pull

### Lever 1: Raise Prices on Existing Clients

This is the highest-leverage, fastest-impact move and almost every agency is under-pricing. A 15% price increase on your existing book, assuming 10% client churn as a result, adds roughly 3.5% to revenue and drops nearly all of it straight to EBITDA.

On a $15M agency at 20% margin, that's an extra ~$500K in EBITDA from a single decision executed over 90 days.

**How to execute:**
- Audit your bottom-quartile margin accounts first. These are the ones to raise (or lose).
- Give 60 days' notice, tied to a scope reset or new service inclusion.
- Frame the increase as indexing to market — not "we need more money."
- Lead with your highest-performing accounts so you have case study proof.

**Mistake to avoid**: Raising rates across the board at the same time. Stagger by tier to manage churn and cash flow.

### Lever 2: Expand Average Account Size

Agencies that sell to holdcos almost always have high average account values. Networks pay premiums for agencies with $500K+ average accounts because it signals enterprise-grade client relationships.

The fastest way to grow account size is to systematically cross-sell adjacent services into your existing book. An SEO agency adds content production. A paid media agency adds creative services. A branding agency adds web design.

**The math**: If your average account is $200K and you can add one $50K retainer to 30% of your accounts, that's a 7.5% revenue bump with near-zero new business cost.

**Mistake to avoid**: Adding services you can't deliver profitably. Every new service line needs a dedicated operator before you sell it.

### Lever 3: Tuck-In Acquisitions

This is the most underused lever by sub-$5M agency owners — and it's exactly how networks themselves grew. Buying a $500K EBITDA agency at 4x gets you $500K in new EBITDA for $2M, which your acquirer will then value at 7-8x when they buy you. You're creating instant multiple arbitrage.

The best tuck-in targets:
- Boutique agencies with 1-2 senior operators and no succession plan
- Complementary service specialists (not direct competitors)
- Owners over 55 who want to stop running the business
- Agencies in your geography or your clients' geography

**Deal structure**: Earnout-heavy deals (30-50% cash at close, rest over 2-3 years tied to performance) protect your downside. Most boutique owners will accept this because they don't have other exit options.

**Mistake to avoid**: Buying an agency you can't integrate. Each tuck-in needs a clear integration plan — shared tech stack, unified client reporting, consolidated back office — or you end up with two agencies under one roof and double the problems.

### Lever 4: Service Productization

Productized services command higher margins than custom work because they're delivered repeatably, scoped tightly, and priced on value instead of hours.

Convert your 2-3 most common engagements into productized packages with fixed scope, fixed price, and documented delivery playbooks. A custom SEO engagement at 20% margin becomes a productized SEO retainer at 40% margin when you control the scope and template the deliverables.

**The math**: If 40% of your revenue shifts from 20% margin custom work to 40% margin productized work, your blended margin jumps from 20% to 28% — which alone takes a $15M agency from $3M to $4.2M EBITDA.

**Mistake to avoid**: Over-productizing. Networks still want to see bespoke, strategic work in your mix. Productize the delivery; keep the strategy custom.

### Lever 5: Operational Margin Expansion

Most agencies have 3-7 points of margin hiding in their cost structure. The usual suspects:

- **Over-staffed middle management**: The classic agency trap. One senior strategist with two juniors often outperforms three mid-level people.
- **Tool sprawl**: $50K-$200K/year typically hides in duplicate and unused SaaS subscriptions.
- **Under-utilization**: Aim for 70-75% utilization on billable staff. Every 5 points below that is real margin walking out the door.
- **Real estate**: If you're still paying for a Class A office and your team works hybrid, that's pure margin you're lighting on fire.
- **Offshore leverage**: Shifting 20-30% of production work to offshore talent with proper QA adds 3-5 points of margin on affected projects.

**Mistake to avoid**: Cost cuts that hurt delivery quality. Buyers see client churn in diligence. Every unhappy client costs you multiple points on your multiple.

### Lever 6: Recurring Revenue Concentration

Networks pay significantly higher multiples for retainer-heavy agencies versus project-heavy ones. A 7x multiple is common for agencies with 70%+ retainer revenue; 4-5x is typical for project-heavy shops.

If you're at 40-50% retainer revenue today, a focused 12-month push to get to 70%+ can move your valuation multiple by 1.5-2x — which often matters more than the absolute EBITDA number.

**How to execute:**
- Convert winning one-off projects into ongoing retainers at engagement close
- Restructure pricing so retainers are dramatically better value than projects
- Fire (or at least de-prioritize) project-only clients below a revenue threshold
- Build service tiers that pull clients into retainer relationships

**Mistake to avoid**: Calling project work "retainer revenue" in your financials. Buyers see through this immediately and it destroys trust in the entire deal.

### Lever 7: Reduce Owner Dependence

This doesn't directly add EBITDA — but it's the thing that prevents your EBITDA number from getting discounted 30-50% during diligence.

Every $1 of EBITDA that depends on the owner personally selling or delivering is worth a fraction of $1 of EBITDA that flows through a repeatable system. Networks explicitly quantify this as "key person risk" and they will aggressively discount agencies where the founder owns client relationships, the sales pipeline, or creative direction.

**What to put in place 12+ months before sale:**
- A named #2 with their own P&L responsibility
- Client relationships owned by account directors, not you
- A pipeline that produces inbound leads without your personal involvement
- Documented SOPs for the work that currently lives in your head
- At least one major account win during the period without your direct involvement

**Mistake to avoid**: Waiting until you're in a sale process to do this. Buyers can smell when an owner suddenly "stepped back" three months ago.

## Putting It Together: A Realistic 18-Month Plan

Here's what this looks like when you sequence the levers properly. Starting from a $15M / $3M EBITDA agency with 20% margins and 50% retainer revenue:

**Months 1-3: Quick wins**
- Price increase on existing accounts → +$500K EBITDA
- Cut tool sprawl and consolidate vendors → +$150K EBITDA
- Tighten utilization by 5 points → +$300K EBITDA
- **Running total**: $3.95M EBITDA

**Months 4-9: Structural improvements**
- Productize top 3 engagements → +$400K EBITDA
- Cross-sell adjacent services into top 20% of accounts → +$300K EBITDA
- Identify and sign first tuck-in acquisition → adds $400K EBITDA
- **Running total**: $5.05M EBITDA

**Months 10-18: Quality multiplication**
- Push retainer revenue from 50% to 70%+
- Document and transfer all key relationships off the owner
- Build out named #2 and senior leadership team
- Hire an M&A-ready CFO (even if fractional)
- Get audited financials for at least one full year

By month 18, you're not just at $5M EBITDA — you're at $5M EBITDA **that's actually sellable at a 7-8x multiple** because you built the other things buyers pay for.

## The Mistakes That Kill Network Deals

Even with $5M EBITDA, networks walk from agencies that have:

1. **Customer concentration over 20%**: One client over 20% of revenue is a deal-breaker. Fix this before diligence.
2. **Working capital problems**: If AR is older than 60 days on average, buyers will factor it into a reduced purchase price.
3. **Unadjusted EBITDA that doesn't match adjusted EBITDA**: Every add-back needs supporting documentation. "Owner salary" yes. "Vague consulting fees" no.
4. **Missing management reporting**: If you can't produce clean monthly P&Ls, service-line margins, and utilization reports on demand, the deal will stall.
5. **Undocumented IP**: Buyers want to know what proprietary methods, platforms, or data assets they're buying. If it all lives in the founder's head, it's not worth anything.

## When to Bring in an Advisor

Start talking to an M&A advisor who specializes in agency sales at least 12 months before you want to sell. Not to run the deal — to benchmark you against what buyers actually want and identify the gaps.

A good advisor will tell you things like:

- "Your margins look fine but your service mix will discount your multiple by 1.5x."
- "Your $3M EBITDA will be adjusted down to $2.4M by any serious buyer. Here's why."
- "Networks aren't buying this service line right now. You should pivot or sell to a different buyer pool."

The difference between a 5x and 8x multiple on $5M EBITDA is $15M in your pocket. Getting the last mile of preparation right is worth every hour you spend on it.

## Final Thoughts

The journey from $3M to $5M EBITDA isn't about one heroic move. It's the compounding effect of seven levers, pulled in the right order, over 18-24 months, with a clear buyer profile in mind from day one.

The agencies that successfully sell to networks aren't the ones with the biggest growth stories. They're the ones with the cleanest financials, the least owner dependence, the highest recurring revenue, and the clearest service focus.

Start with pricing. Then tighten operations. Then productize. Then acquire. Then professionalize the management. Then sell.

Do it in that order, and your $3M EBITDA turns into a $35-40M exit instead of a $12-15M one.
